# Writing LinkedIn & professional social posts

A LinkedIn post competes for attention in a fast-moving feed. Unlike a document,
nobody *has* to read it — you earn each second. The goals are to stop the scroll,
deliver one worthwhile idea, and invite a response. Craft and restraint beat
volume and hype.

## Win the hook (the first line or two)

The feed shows only the first ~1-2 lines before a "…more" cutoff. That's your
whole audition. If the hook doesn't land, nothing else you wrote matters.

A good hook is short (aim under ~12 words), specific, and creates a reason to keep
reading. Reliable angles:

- **A surprising result or number** — *"We cut onboarding from 3 weeks to 3 days.
  Here's the one change that did it."*
- **A pointed question** — *"Why do great engineers so often quit within a year?"*
- **A contrarian take** — *"Most 'best practices' are just habits nobody has
  questioned."*
- **A small, honest confession or scene** — *"I lost a $2M deal because of one
  email. What it taught me:"*

Avoid fake cliffhangers and bait ("You won't BELIEVE what happened next"). Curiosity
is good; manipulation reads as spam and ages badly.

## Structure for the feed

- **Short lines, generous whitespace.** One or two sentences per paragraph. Walls
  of text die on mobile.
- **One idea per post.** A post is not an essay. Make a single point well; save the
  rest for next time.
- **Tell, then land.** A short story or concrete example, then the takeaway or
  lesson. People remember specifics and stories, not abstractions.
- **End where the point lands.** Stop once you've made your point. If a question
  genuinely invites a useful reply, ask one — but a reflex "What do you think?" on
  every post is its own cliché. One invitation at most, never three.

One workable shape, *not* a mandatory template, is **hook → short story or insight
→ the takeaway**, sometimes closing on a question. Vary it. If every post you write
has the identical skeleton, readers feel the formula even when they can't name it.

## Voice

Write like a smart person talking, not a press release. First person, conversational,
and specific to the author's real experience. Credibility comes from concrete detail
and genuine perspective, not from buzzwords or borrowed authority. Read it aloud; if
it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

Write only from what the author actually did, thinks, and knows. A specific story or
number is powerful, but never invent one, sharpen an opinion the author doesn't hold,
or dramatize a scene that didn't happen just to manufacture a hook. If you don't have
the raw material, ask for it. A hollow post that reads well still rings false.

## Hashtags and tagging

- Use a few (roughly 2-5) relevant hashtags, not a pile. They aid discovery, not
  reach-hacking.
- Tag people or companies only when they're genuinely relevant and you'd be
  comfortable with them seeing it. Gratuitous tagging annoys people.

## Traps to avoid

- **Broetry / over-formatting.** One-line-per-paragraph theatrics and emoji dividers
  now read as formulaic. Use whitespace to serve reading, not to perform.
- **Engagement bait.** "Comment 'YES' if you agree" and follow-for-follow gimmicks
  cheapen the post and the author.
- **Humblebragging and vague inspiration.** Say something concrete and useful. If a
  post could be posted by anyone about anything, it says nothing.
- **Burying the idea.** If your one worthwhile point is in the last line, move it up
  or into the hook.

## Before / after

- Before: *"I'm excited to share that I recently had the opportunity to attend a
  wonderful conference where I learned so many valuable insights about leadership
  and I'm truly humbled and grateful for the experience. #blessed #leadership
  #growth #mindset #inspiration"*
- After:
  > The best leadership advice I heard this week came from a janitor, not a keynote.
  >
  > "I don't clean floors. I keep the hospital running."
  >
  > Same job. Completely different ownership.
  >
  > I stopped asking how to motivate people. I started asking what story they tell
  > about their work.
  >
  > #leadership #culture

The rewrite opens with a hook, tells one concrete story, and lands on a single
point, in a fraction of the words. Note it doesn't end on a reflex question; the
insight carries the close on its own.
